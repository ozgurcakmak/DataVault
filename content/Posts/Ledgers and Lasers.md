When I read a comic or a science-fiction novel - or any other speculative media - I think about its logistics: Who builds the battleships? Who trains the crew? Who signs the shipping manifest? 

Most speculative universes never answer this. They’ll show you that gleaming ,big honking dreadnought or the planet-killing superweapon, but they won’t show you the factory floor that forged it, or the academy that produced its captain, or the supply chain that keeps it fueled. And once your brain starts asking about the plumbing, it’s hard to stop.

*Warhammer 40k* is one of the few that at least tries to answer. On the Imperium side, there are forge worlds churning out battleships, agri-worlds feeding the war machine, and Schola Progeniums training the next generation of officers. Space is busy in a way that feels lived-in. The answers aren’t definitive - step over to the Chaos side and things get very murky, very fast (because magic. When magic enters into the picture all becomes fair) but you can tell someone spent brain labor on the question. The weight is there.

*Star Trek*, by contrast, often forgets the question entirely. The Federation gets a pass; we know there’s a Starfleet, and we can infer an Earth-based infrastructure behind it. The replicator "technology" is another way of saying magic, as it creates something out of nothing. But the moment you encounter an alien civilization, the illusion shatters. Take the Kazon in *Voyager*. Their planetside society is feudal at best. They barely manage agriculture, let alone warp theory. But they’re cruising around in full fleets of starships. Who built them? Where? If there’s a spaceport, how did a feudal culture build the first drydock? The show drops the ships into frame and hopes you won’t ask.

And logistics isn’t the only place where the worldbuilding feels thin in Star Trek. Why does every planet function as a single, uniform culture? Sure, some episodes feature an internal crisis, but we almost never see a captain negotiating with a messy coalition of nation-states. The planet becomes one character with one accent, which is convenient for the plot but makes the universe feel stage-lit and small.

*Battletech* at least offers a fig leaf. It tells us the Star League built everything centuries ago, and the current factions are just fighting over the leftovers. Maintenance survives through inherited knowledge, even though modern tech can’t manufacture new starships. It’s not a great answer. I mean, if you can keep a fusion reactor running for three hundred years, you can probably reverse-engineer the damn thing but at least there is an answer. The writers acknowledged the audit.

For me, that unanswered question is a slow poison. It doesn’t ruin the story immediately, but it leaks into the suspension of disbelief, one drop at a time. The second I start wondering about shipyards and supply chains, I’m no longer inside the adventure; I’m stuck in the lobby, checking the writers’ math.
Maybe I’m just cursed with a brain that wants ledgers with its lasers. But I think the best speculative fiction makes you feel the gravity of the world; not just the hero’s sword, but the thousand hands that forged it. When a universe has that weight, the magic doesn’t break. It holds.
